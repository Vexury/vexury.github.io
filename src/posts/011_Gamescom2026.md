I have been to Gamescom a few times as a visitor (which mostly means queueing).
This year I had an exhibitor badge.
I went to Cologne with the Midain Studios team to show Scarlet O'Beetle and the Unseen World, the game I do shader and rendering work on as a contractor.
We had a small booth in the Romanian pavilion, and I did not know what to expect from the other side of the counter.

## The Booth

Our booth was small: one screen with the game running, the poster, a counter and more ivy than strictly necessary.
People kept stopping anyway, and almost all of them started with some version of "so what is this?" (fair enough, the first thing you see is a ladybug in armor).

<div style="text-align: center; margin: 1.5rem 0">
  <img src="{% thumb '/images/Gamescom2026/booth.jpg', 900 %}" alt="The Scarlet O'Beetle booth in the Romanian pavilion" style="max-width:60%" loading="lazy">
</div>

So we explained it over and over, and it went down well.
There was a steady stream of people who leaned in to look at the art and stayed for the pitch (I can't point to one standout moment).
At some point I noticed that I had dropped the sentences nobody reacted to and kept the ones that got a nod. So the booth was also a way to test the pitch on a few hundred strangers.

## The Business Area

The badge was the main reason to be there.
The business area is quieter than the public halls and everybody in it is there to talk.

<div style="text-align: center; margin: 1.5rem 0">
  <img src="{% thumb '/images/Gamescom2026/pass.jpg', 400 %}" alt="Exhibitor pass" style="max-width:25%" loading="lazy">
</div>

Most of my conversations were with people in roughly my situation: other indie developers and freelancers with a project going on and no clear idea yet how to make it pay.
Those conversations were easy because we all have the same problems.

Two meetings were about funding. One was with Games BW, the games branch of the state of Baden-Wuerttemberg, and the other with the DLR project management agency that runs the federal games funding program.
I had a fuzzy idea that these programs exist and assumed they are not for a one-person studio. Both explained to me in some detail at which point they are.
I left with a page of notes.

The conversation I keep coming back to was with a co-founder of Keen Games. They ship Enshrouded on their own engine, and he was open about what that looks like day to day and what it takes to keep a custom tech stack alive under a live game.
I [have a small Vulkan engine](/posts/003_Engine/) of my own (mostly because I enjoy it), so hearing how that decision plays out at a real studio was the best hour of the week.

I also talked to ArenaNet about Guild Wars 3. Nothing I can repeat here, but it was a good chat.

## The Public Halls

Whenever someone else was at the booth, I went out into the public halls.
HoYoverse had the most elaborate setup of the show again, and I got to play Path of Exile at the Grinding Gear Games booth (in a hall with several thousand other people).

I spent the most time at the Indie Arena Booth. It has dozens of indie games in one area, some finished, some obviously still a year out, and next to each one a developer who is happy to talk.
I played a lot and asked a lot of questions.
After a few days behind my own counter I had more sympathy for the people behind theirs, and I think my questions got better because of it.

## Takeaways

The booth was fun, but I underestimated how much happens in the business area.
I spent the first two days figuring that out instead of having meetings booked already. Next time I will write to people before the show.

The funding talks changed my plans the most. "Apply for funding" was on my list of things to look into eventually, and now it has a date.

The Keen Games conversation also stuck with me. I like building my own engine, and after hearing what it costs to keep one alive under a shipped game, I am a lot more relaxed about mine being a hobby project.

I'll be back in Cologne next year, hopefully with a booth of my own.
